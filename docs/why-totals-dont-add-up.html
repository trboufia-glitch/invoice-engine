Why your invoice totals don't add up

If subtotal + tax ≠ total, the file is wrong — not the spreadsheet, not your arithmetic. There are five causes, and they are easy to tell apart.

In this article
  1. The VAT line was blank and read as zero
  2. The tax rate doesn't match the tax amount
  3. The discount was applied twice, or not at all
  4. European decimals got parsed as thousands separators
  5. The semicolon export lost its delimiters
  6. How to find the offending row in seconds

1. The VAT line was blank and read as zero

What it looks like

The total is short by exactly the tax you expected. Nothing looks broken.

A client sends an invoice where the VAT cell is empty — a data-export fault on their side, or a partial save. Any spreadsheet treats an empty cell as zero, so the invoice quietly under-charges. It passes every visual check and fails only at the return.

This is the most common and most expensive of the five, because the total stays plausible.

Fix: treat an empty cell as missing, never as zero. If total − subtotal ≠ the tax you expect, the tax field is absent — not zero.

2. The stated rate doesn't match the stated amount

What it looks like

The invoice says 20% but the amount is wrong for 20%.

subtotal   1,000.00
VAT 20%      100.00     ← should be 200.00
total       1,100.00   ← internally consistent, externally wrong

The invoice adds up perfectly. It is simply charging the wrong tax, which makes it non-compliant even though it reconciles.

Fix: recompute the rate from the figures — tax ÷ subtotal — and compare it to the rate printed on the document.

3. The discount was applied twice, or not at all

What it looks like

The total is off by a round percentage and the tax looks proportionally wrong too.

Discounts get applied at the line level, at the invoice level, or both — and exports don't always agree about which. When tax is computed on a discounted subtotal but the total subtracts the discount twice, the row no longer reconciles.

Fix: recompute from the line items, not from the stated total. If the sum of lines + tax ≠ total, the discrepancy is in the discount logic.

4. European decimals parsed as thousands separators

What it looks like

Totals are off by a factor of a thousand, and only for some suppliers.

A German or French export writes 1.234,56. A parser that assumes the US convention reads the dot as a thousands separator and gets 1234.56 — or worse, strips punctuation and gets 123456. The same supplier's invoices then fail while everyone else's pass.

Related: 1 234,56 with a space, and the Swiss 1'234.50 with an apostrophe.

Fix: parse on the last separator, not the first. Whichever of . or , comes last is the decimal point.

5. The semicolon export lost its delimiters

What it looks like

Every column has landed in the wrong field — invoice numbers containing dates, totals containing vendor names.

Locales that use a comma as the decimal separator also use a semicolon as the field separator. A parser that hardcodes , reads the entire row as one cell. Nothing errors; you just get garbage that looks structurally valid.

This one is worth checking for specifically, because it silently corrupts every row from that supplier while leaving the file readable.

Fix: detect the delimiter per file before splitting. Count candidates on the first few lines and use the winner.

How to find the offending row

You do not need to inspect anything by hand. The check is one subtraction per row:

subtotal + taxmust equal total, within a small tolerance for rounding
tax ÷ subtotalmust equal the stated rate
due datemust not precede the issue date
issue datemust be a real calendar date

A tolerance of about 0.05 avoids false alarms on rounding. Anything larger is a real discrepancy worth chasing.

What it will not do

  • It does not know your tax rules. It checks internal consistency, not whether the rate is the correct rate for your jurisdiction.
